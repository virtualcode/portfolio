Federated Personal Calendars: Reclaiming Your Schedule

By Michael Perry August 2026

Your calendar is personal. Your schedule, your meetings, your time—it's yours alone. Yet for most of us, that intimate data lives on someone else's servers: Google's, Apple's, Microsoft's. We've traded convenience for custody.

What if you could keep your calendar truly yours—while still sharing and syncing with others? That's the promise of federated personal calendars.

What Is a Federated Personal Calendar?

A federated personal calendar is a calendar system where:

  • Each user runs their own server — You host your calendar on a machine you control, or rent from a provider you trust.
  • Servers talk to each other — When you share an event or invite someone, your server sends a message to their server. They sync via a standard protocol.
  • No central authority — There's no single company storing everyone's data. Calendar data lives distributed across independent servers, all speaking the same language.
  • You keep ownership — Your calendar never leaves your server unless you explicitly share it. You control who sees what, when, and for how long.
Real-world analogy: Email is federated. You have a Gmail account (Google's server), your friend uses ProtonMail (ProtonMail's server), your workplace runs its own mail server. When you email each other, the servers talk via SMTP. No central authority, data stays distributed, but it all works together via a standard protocol.

How It Differs From Other Models

Model How It Works Trade-offs
Federated personal calendar Each user's server talks to others via standard protocol (ActivityPub or similar) You must run a server; discovery is manual ("here's my calendar URL")
Self-hosted single-node You run a server, but people access it only with a shared URL; no inter-server sync Simplest to set up, but no team calendars unless everyone shares one server
SaaS (Google, Apple, Microsoft) One company's servers store everyone's data Easy to use, but you trust one company with intimate schedule data; they own your data
Peer-to-peer Devices sync directly (like BitTorrent); no servers No server to maintain, but hard to invite people you don't already know

The Current Gap: Why Federated Calendars Don't Exist Yet

Here's the surprising part: there is no production federated personal calendar today.

Federated social media works—Mastodon is federated social software. Federated event organizers exist—Mobilizon and Gancio let communities share and organize events. But neither of these solves personal calendar federation.

Why? Because calendars are harder than they look.

The Hard Problems

Recurring event conflicts: If you and I both edit the same recurring meeting on our own servers, then sync, the system sees two divergent versions. "Weekly meeting every Monday at 10am" has been edited by both of us in different ways. There's no clean way to merge these edits—a problem known as the RRULE conflict. No production calendar has solved this with user happiness in mind.

Timezone drift: Events store a start time and timezone, but clients quietly rewrite floating times when daylight saving time changes. Your "10am every Monday" silently shifts twice a year. Across federated servers, this becomes a ghost problem nobody sees until meetings are missed.

Attendee privacy leaks: When one person RSVPs to a meeting, that response needs to reach the organizer. But in federated settings, even encrypted calendar data leaks attendance graphs and timing information to intermediate servers—enough to infer who's in meetings together.

Key recovery for encrypted calendars: If a calendar is encrypted end-to-end (so even the server can't read it), and you lose your password, your entire calendar is gone. There's no usable way to recover it, and no production system has solved social recovery securely.

"Federated personal calendars are effectively unsolved. No production system federates VEVENT-like data so that 'next Tuesday 3pm' surfaces in a peer's calendar via a standard protocol. That's the real opening."

Why This Matters

Your calendar is a map of your life. Where you work, when you sleep, who you spend time with, what you prioritize. In the hands of a data broker, this information is valuable. Employers use it to track you. Marketers use it to predict your behavior. Governments request it. Insurance companies might price you differently based on your patterns.

With a federated calendar, you own that data. You decide who sees it. You can move your calendar to a different server anytime. You can delete it with no company holding a copy. You can encrypt it so even your server operator can't read it.

The technology already exists in pieces:

  • CalDAV servers like Radicale and Xandikos can store calendar data in standard formats
  • CRDTs (Automerge, Yjs) handle real-time sync across distributed systems
  • ActivityPub is a proven federation protocol (used by Mastodon, PeerTube)
  • JSCalendar (RFC 8984) is a modern, machine-friendly calendar standard
  • End-to-end encryption libraries are mature (libsodium, TweetNaCl)

What's missing is a cohesive system that ties these pieces together and solves the hard problems: RRULE merging, timezone correctness, attendee privacy, and key recovery.

The Opportunity

For developers and technologists, federated personal calendars represent an unsolved, well-defined problem. It's the kind of gap that startups and open-source communities thrive in—real user pain, existing building blocks, and no dominant incumbent solution.

For users, it's a question: How much do you value owning your time? If your answer is "everything," then a federated calendar isn't just nice to have—it's necessary.

Interested in calendar technology, privacy, or distributed systems? I'm exploring these questions in a project called coolcal—a design-driven dive into what a production federated calendar would look like. Feel free to reach out if this resonates.

Next Steps

If you want to experiment with calendar federation today, the closest you can get is:

  • Radicale or Xandikos: Self-hosted CalDAV servers that work with standard calendar clients
  • Mobilizon or Gancio: Federated event organizers (shared community calendars, not personal)
  • Etebase: Open-source E2EE calendar backend, self-hostable

None of these fully solve the federated personal calendar problem yet. But they're getting closer.
